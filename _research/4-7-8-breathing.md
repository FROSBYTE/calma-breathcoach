# Research: /4-7-8-breathing/

Research date: 2026-11-08. Numbers in [brackets] refer to the numbered list in "Sources". Research stopped early at the coordinator's request, and the web-search budget ran out before Reddit and Quora could be checked. Gaps are marked NOT RESEARCHED and unconfirmed claims are marked UNVERIFIED.

## Summary

- 4-7-8 (Weil also calls it the "Relaxing Breath") means breathing in through the nose for 4 counts, holding for 7 and breathing out through the mouth with a "whoosh" for 8. Andrew Weil, MD popularized it, and it comes from yogic pranayama [1][2][7][8].
- Weil's own rules: the 4:7:8 *ratio* matters, not the number of seconds. Do 4 breaths per session, at least twice a day. Do no more than 4 breaths at a time in the first month, then go up to 8 if you want. Mild lightheadedness at first is normal [1][2].
- Studies on 4-7-8 itself are few, small and mostly short. The best of them is a randomized trial of 90 patients after bariatric surgery (less anxiety) [21]. There is also a small tinnitus RCT (sleep, stress and anxiety scores improved) [22], plus single-session and uncontrolled studies [20][24][29].
- No study found here measured the claim that 4-7-8 "puts you to sleep in 60 seconds". The claim comes from 2015 press headlines [18][19]. Weil's own handout says the effect is "subtle when you first try it but gains in power with repetition and practice" [1].
- In the only head-to-head test found, breathing at 6 breaths per minute raised heart rate variability (HRV) more than 4-7-8 or box breathing did [27]. The evidence for slow breathing in general is stronger than the evidence for 4-7-8 specifically [33][34][35].

## What it is and origin

- 4-7-8 is a paced breathing pattern: inhale for 4 counts, hold for 7, exhale for 8 [7][8].
- Cleveland Clinic says it was "popularized in 2015 by integrative medicine specialist Andrew Weil, MD" and that it "has ancient roots in the yogic practice of pranayama" [7].
- Medical News Today calls it "a form of pranayama, which is the practice of breath regulation" [8]. Sleep Foundation calls it "rooted in pranayama" [11].
- Weil founded the Andrew Weil Center for Integrative Medicine at the University of Arizona [6]. The center says the technique was "developed by Dr. Andrew Weil" and is part of its core curriculum [5].
- Weil's handout is titled "The 4-7-8 (or Relaxing Breath) Exercise" [2]. A hospital copy describes it as "A patient handout from Dr. Andrew Weil" [1].
- Date Weil first taught or published it, and the specific pranayama technique it came from: UNVERIFIED. One third-party copy says it was shared from Weil's site in September 2014 [2].
- Note: drweil.com blocked automated access, so pages [3] and [4] could not be read. Weil's wording below is taken from reproductions [1][2].

## Exact steps and timings

Weil's instructions, quoted from the Hunterdon Health handout [1] and the third-party copy [2]:

1. Position: "Ideally, sit with your back straight." [1] Also: "Although you can do the exercise in any position, sit with your back straight while learning the exercise." [2]
2. Tongue: "Place the tip of your tongue against the ridge of tissue just behind your upper front teeth, and keep it there through the entire exercise." [1]
3. Exhale around the tongue: "Exhale through your mouth around your tongue; try pursing your lips slightly if this seems awkward." [1]
4. "Exhale completely through your mouth, making a whoosh sound." [1]
5. "Close your mouth and inhale quietly through your nose to a mental count of four." [2]
6. "Hold your breath for a count of seven." [2]
7. "Exhale completely through your mouth, making a whoosh sound to a count of eight." [2]
8. "This is one breath. Now inhale again and repeat the cycle three more times for a total of four breaths." [1]

Counts are a ratio, not seconds:
- "The absolute time you spend on each phase is not important; the ratio of 4:7:8 is important. If you have trouble holding your breath, speed the exercise up but keep to the ratio of 4:7:8 for the three phases. With practice you can slow it all down…" [1]
- "Exhalation takes twice as long as inhalation." [2]

How often and how to build up:
- "Do it at least twice a day. You cannot do it too frequently. Do not do more than four breaths at one time for the first month of practice. Later, if you wish, you can extend it to eight breaths." [1]
- When to use it: "Use it whenever anything upsetting happens - before you react. Use it whenever you are aware of internal tension. Use it to help you fall asleep." [1]

How other sources differ (a gap the copy could address):
- Medical News Today [8], Healthline [9], Sleep Foundation [11] and the Weil center's own magazine [5] all write the counts as *seconds*.
- Medical News Today offers a faster version: 2 seconds in, 3.5 hold, 4 out [8].
- Cleveland Clinic's tips say to do "three cycles twice a day", but its steps say four cycles [7].

Timing arithmetic (calculated here, not taken from a source):
- If counts equal seconds, one breath takes 19 s, which is about 3.16 breaths per minute (the same figure appears in [17]).
- Four breaths therefore take about 76 s.

## How it works

Plain-language explanation, drawn from general research on breathing rather than tests of 4-7-8 itself:

- **Breathing out slows the heart.** Heart rate naturally rises slightly when you breathe in and falls when you breathe out. This is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia [37][38].
- **Breathing out activates the vagus nerve.** Vagus nerve activity "is suppressed during inhalation and facilitated during exhalation and slow respiration cycles" [37]. The vagus nerve is the main nerve of the body's calming "rest and digest" (parasympathetic) system [13].
- **A longer exhale seems to matter more than a longer inhale.** One cited study found higher HRV during slow breathing only when the exhale was extended, not the inhale [37]. In 4-7-8 the exhale is twice as long as the inhale [2].
- **The hold.** "Extending, slowing and holding respiration are all considered vagal maneuvers" [37]. One review lists *proposed* (not proven) mechanisms for 4-7-8 [32]:
  - a slow pace of about 3 breaths per minute
  - a low inhale-to-exhale ratio
  - possibly less chemoreceptor stimulation during the hold
- **Counting gives the mind a job.** "The counting sequence is a way to focus your mind on something other than your worries." (Melissa Young, MD, Cleveland Clinic) [7]
- **Practice may matter.** Dr. Young says, "the more we do it, the more we allow our bodies to go into that parasympathetic mode" [7]. Weil says the effect "gains in power with repetition and practice" [1].
- **Oxygen.** The common claim that the hold "fills the blood with oxygen" [18][23] is UNVERIFIED and is not supported by any physiology source checked here.
- **Lightheadedness.** The reason is UNVERIFIED. Cleveland Clinic links it to breathing more slowly [7]. A non-medical site says it comes from shifting carbon dioxide [17].

## Benefits and evidence

### Evidence for 4-7-8 specifically (all small studies, often from a single centre)

| Claim | Study | Design and size | Finding | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Less anxiety after surgery | Aktaş & İlgin 2023 [21] | RCT, 90 bariatric surgery patients (30 deep breathing / 30 4-7-8 / 30 control) | 4-7-8 group had lower post-test state anxiety than both other groups. Only the deep-breathing group improved in quality of life | Moderate-low (one RCT) |
| Less anxiety before endoscopy | Parlak 2026 [30] | RCT, 75 older adults (25 per group) | Fear and anxiety fell with 4-7-8 and with a stress ball. The stress ball reduced anxiety more | Low-moderate |
| Tinnitus, sleep, stress | Kirazli 2026 [22] | RCT, 23 vs 25 analysed. 4 cycles twice daily for 6 weeks | 4-7-8 group: Insomnia Severity Index 8.39 → 4.35, Perceived Stress 14.26 → 11.61, trait anxiety 38.87 → 34.22. Control group did not change significantly. Groups differed in anxiety at baseline. No blinding of participants | Low-moderate |
| Heart rate and blood pressure | Vierra 2022 [20] | One session, non-randomized, 43 young adults, no non-breathing control | Both groups had lower heart rate and systolic blood pressure afterwards (p < 0.05). HRV results were mixed | Low |
| HRV compared with other techniques | Marchant 2025 [27] | 84 students, repeated measures | "Breathing at 6 bpm increased HRV measures more than square or 4-7-8 breathing." No meaningful change in blood pressure or mood under any condition | Moderate (direct comparison) |
| Stress and mood | Avcık 2026 [28] | Crossover RCT, 34 students | 4-7-8 lowered stress and negative mood in women. "No significant main effects of condition" on HRV | Low |
| COPD anxiety and depression | Pandekar 2019 [23] | 87 randomized by lottery, 4 days | Less breathlessness and lower anxiety/depression (HADS) vs usual care. The paper itself contains errors, e.g. it says "holding the breath for a count of 8" | Low |
| COPD sleep | Satria 2025 [24] | 45 patients, no control group | Mean score 13.33 → 4.93 (presumably PSQI sleep quality) | Very low |
| Sleep in students | Doğan, nursing students [25][26] | RCT, 82 enrolled, 4 weeks, practised 30 min before bed | Reported to improve PSQI sleep quality. Exact figures UNVERIFIED (full paper not accessible) | Unknown |
| Pain after knee replacement | Eskimez 2025 [29] | One group, before/after only | Pain score 7.25 → 6.27 right after → 3.84 at 30 min | Very low |
| Hemodialysis pain and sleep | Güvener & Koç 2025 [31] | RCT, 69 patients | Improved, but 4-7-8 was combined with 3 other techniques, so the effect cannot be attributed to 4-7-8 | Not attributable |

- A 2025 review lists only two 4-7-8 studies and notes "small sample size" as a limitation of both [32].
- Medical News Today: "There is limited clinical research to support these claims about 4-7-8 breathing" [8].
- Claims about cravings, anger and migraine [8][16]: no 4-7-8 study found. UNVERIFIED.

### Evidence for slow or controlled breathing in general (stronger, but not specific to 4-7-8)

- **Fincham 2023** [33], a meta-analysis of RCTs. Breathwork vs control:
  - stress g = −0.35 (12 RCTs, 785 people)
  - anxiety g = −0.32
  - depression g = −0.40
  - most studies were at moderate risk of bias
- **Zaccaro 2018** [34], a systematic review of 15 studies of breathing below 10 breaths per minute. Slow breathing increased HRV and reduced anxiety and arousal. The authors call the evidence "interesting albeit limited".
- **Bentley 2023** [35], a systematic review of 58 studies. Effective practices "avoided fast-only breath paces and sessions <5 min".
  - Note: Weil's 4-breath session takes about 1–1.5 min (calculated here).
- **Magnon 2021** [36]: one 5-minute slow-breathing session raised vagal tone (HF power) and lowered state anxiety in 47 adults. There was no control group.

## 4-7-8 for sleep

- **What Weil says:** "Use it to help you fall asleep." [1]
- **What other sources say:**
  - Sleep Foundation: the pacing "helps slow your heart rate, quiet the mind, and prepare your body for sleep". Its comparison table lists 4-7-8 as best for "Anxiety", taking "2-5 minutes" [11].
  - Healthline: regular practice "could also help some people fall asleep in a shorter period of time". It suggests lying down when using it for sleep, and seeing a doctor for frequent or chronic insomnia [9].
  - Cleveland Clinic: links better sleep to less anxiety and cites the heart rate and blood pressure study [7][20].
- **Sleep evidence:**
  - Tinnitus RCT: insomnia scores improved [22].
  - COPD study with no control group [24].
  - Nursing-student RCT with UNVERIFIED figures [25][26].
  - None of these measured how long it takes to fall asleep with objective tools such as sleep-lab recordings (none found).
- **Fact-check of "fall asleep in 60 seconds":** not supported.
  - Where it came from: HuffPost UK (5 May 2015) wrote "Dr Andrew Weil says the 4-7-8 breathing technique can help us fall asleep in just 60 seconds" and cited no study [18]. Fox 5 DC (6 May 2015) attributed the story to the Daily Mail [19]. A 2021 University of Arizona Health Sciences headline repeats it [6].
  - Whether Weil himself said "60 seconds": UNVERIFIED. His handout does not say it [1][2].
  - The claim that he said 6–8 weeks of practice leads to 60-second sleep: UNVERIFIED (the source page returned 404).
  - Medical News Today reports it only as "Some proponents claim…" [8].
  - Four breaths take about 76 counts, so "60 seconds" probably describes how long the exercise lasts, not how fast it works [17].
- **Suggested angle for the copy:** a wind-down routine at bedtime, not a guaranteed sleep switch. Insomnia that persists needs a clinician [9].

## Safety

- **Lightheadedness is common at first.**
  - Weil: "If you feel a little lightheaded when you first breathe this way, do not be concerned - it will pass." [1]
  - Cleveland Clinic: beginners "may experience a little bit of lightheadedness or shortness of breath" [7].
- **Sit or lie down.** Medical News Today: "it is advisable to try this technique when sitting or lying down to prevent dizziness or falls." If lightheaded, stop or shorten the breaths [8]. Healthline: take a break [9].
- **Don't strain.**
  - Cleveland Clinic: hold "without straining" [7].
  - Weil: if the hold is hard, speed up the counts but keep the ratio [1].
  - Counting slow "seconds" can make the 7-count hold long and uncomfortable. The ratio rule is the fix [1].
- **Limit breaths per session.** Up to 4 breaths per session in the first month [1].
- **Who should check with a doctor.** No medical body's guidance specific to 4-7-8 was found (NOT RESEARCHED further). Nearby guidance:
  - Breath-hold techniques "may not be suitable for people who find this difficult". People with high blood pressure or who are pregnant should speak with a doctor (Medical News Today, on box breathing) [14].
  - People with COPD, asthma or anxiety should talk to their provider (Cleveland Clinic, on diaphragmatic breathing) [15].
  - Lists of pregnancy, heart or lung conditions, epilepsy, glaucoma and panic disorder come only from non-clinical sites [16][17]: UNVERIFIED.
  - "Breath holds can make panic worse for some people" [17]: UNVERIFIED.
  - Don't practise in or near water or while driving [17] (non-clinical source).
- **Not a treatment on its own.** Breathing "shouldn't be the only treatment" [15].

## Common mistakes and tips

- **Treating counts as fixed seconds.** The ratio is what matters [1].
- **Doing too many rounds too early.** Stick to 4 breaths for the first month [1].
- **Breathing the wrong way.** "You always inhale quietly through your nose and exhale audibly through your mouth" [2].
- **Skipping the first full "whoosh" exhale** [1].
- **Letting the tongue drift.** Keep it in place the whole time, and purse the lips if the exhale feels awkward [1]. Whether the tongue position changes the effect has no evidence either way [17]: UNVERIFIED.
- **Expecting instant results.** The effect is "subtle when you first try it" [1]. Benefits may show "after several days or weeks" [8].
- **Using it only in a crisis.** Practise when calm, and tie practice to two daily routines (Dr. Young) [7].
- **Straining on the hold** [7].
- **Practising standing.** Sit or lie down [8]. Sit with a straight back while learning, and lie down at bedtime [1][9].
- **Tip:** keep sessions consistent, once or twice a day [1][8].

## Comparisons

| Technique | Pattern | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 4-7-8 | 4 in / 7 hold / 8 out, exhale through the mouth. About 3.16 breaths per minute if counts are seconds | See the evidence table above. Raised HRV less than 6 breaths per minute [27] |
| Box (square, sama vritti) | 4 in / 4 hold / 4 out / 4 hold. Used by US Navy SEALs [13] | Box arm (n=21) was not significantly better than mindfulness [38]. Raised HRV less than 6 breaths per minute [27]. WebMD: 4-7-8 is linked to relaxation, box breathing "is said to also help improve concentration" [10] |
| Diaphragmatic (belly) | Slow belly breathing, no fixed counts. 5–10 min, 3–4 times a day [15] | Bariatric RCT: deep breathing improved quality of life, while 4-7-8 lowered anxiety more [21] |
| Cyclic sighing | Two inhales (the second a top-up), then a long slow exhale, no hold. 5 min a day [38] | Breathwork, "especially the exhale-focused cyclic sighing", improved mood more than mindfulness (n=108, 1 month) [38]. Never tested against 4-7-8 |
| 6 breaths per minute (resonance) | For example 4 s in / 6 s out | Biggest HRV increase, but with "mild over-breathing" [27] |
| NHS calm breathing | In 1–5, out 1–5, at least 5 min, no hold [12] | A gentle option for people who dislike breath holds |

Search-led verdict for "4-7-8 vs box breathing": both are used for stress, and neither is proven better for mood. The longer exhale is the main difference [2][13][27].

| Myth | Fact |

| It puts you to sleep in 60 seconds | No study found has measured this. The claim comes from 2015 headlines [8][18][19] |
| The counts must be seconds | Weil says the ratio matters, not the seconds [1] |
| More rounds work better | Weil sets a limit of 4 breaths for the first month [1] |
| It's scientifically proven | The research is limited and small [8][32] |
| It beats other breathing for HRV | 6 breaths per minute did better [27] |
