# Research: /visual-breathing/

Research date: 2026-11-08. Source numbers in [brackets] refer to the list in "Sources". Research was stopped early on the coordinator's instruction, so gaps are marked NOT RESEARCHED and claims I could not check are marked UNVERIFIED.

## Summary

- A visual breathing guide (also called a breath pacer) is a moving shape or object that sets the rhythm: you breathe in as it grows or rises and out as it shrinks or falls. Charity and hospital-linked pages say it removes the need to count and gives the eyes "something gentle to focus on" [14][15][13][16].
- Slow breathing in general has reasonable but modest evidence. A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (785 adults) found a small-to-medium effect on stress (g = -0.35), but most studies were at moderate risk of bias [2]. Lab studies that paced breathing with an on-screen moving ball at 6 breaths/min found raised HRV (RMSSD) [6][9].
- Evidence on **visual** pacing compared with other pacing is thin and mixed. In one study 86% of people found a metronome harder to follow than visual cues [10]. A small study found audio guidance more calming than visual [11]. A 2014 app study reported that visualisation improved on audio-only instructions (details UNVERIFIED) [12].
- Shape techniques (square/box, triangle, star, lazy 8 / figure 8, rectangle, hexagon, heart, hand/five-finger, breathing ball) are mostly taught to children by school counsellors, OT practitioners and teachers. Patterns differ between sources [17][18][19][20][21][22][23][24].
- Safety: slow breathing is low-risk ("few adverse effects expected") [5]. Still, people can overbreathe and feel faint [8]. About 15% of one student sample reported relaxation-induced anxiety [28]. Breath focus may not suit people with breathing problems or heart failure [25].

## What it is and why it helps

**Definition.**
- A breath pacer is a tool "designed to guide your breathing patterns". Visual pacers use light or moving shapes, auditory pacers use sound, and tactile pacers use vibration or pressure. This comes from a commercial device maker [33].
- In HRV biofeedback clinics, the standard screen includes a moving ball. Shaffer & Meehan describe a display "with the moving yellow ball [that] is designed to help clients breathe at 6 bpm" [8].
- Research labs use the same idea. In You et al. 2021, participants inhaled through the nose as a small ball rose and exhaled through pursed lips as it fell: 4 s in, 6 s out, 6 cycles/min [9].

**The common convention: breathe in as the shape grows, out as it shrinks.**
- TLC for Kids: "As the animation expands, breathe in." "As it slowly contracts, breathe out." [15]
- The UK charity No Panic's animation tells users to "Breathe in when the animation gets bigger" [38].
- Children's Wisconsin uses the same rule with a physical Hoberman sphere [13].

**Why people use it (only where a source says so):**
- *No counting needed.* "There is no need to count or worry about timing" [14]. "Without counting, timing or remembering a breathing pattern ... no pressure to breathe perfectly" [15].
- *Easier to follow than sound (one study).* "A majority of subjects (86%) perceived that breathing to auditory cues was more difficult compared with visual cues" (metronome vs scrolling waveform, n = 20) [10].
- *An anchor for attention.* The breathing ball "serves as a focus, or an anchor, for mindful breathing" [13]. "Having something visual to focus your breathing on can be a useful tool to calm down in the moment" (University of Pittsburgh / UPMC project) [16]. Figure 8 tracing gives the eyes and mind "something gentle to focus on" [14].
- *Kids keep track more easily.* Shapes are "a more playful way to help them keep track of their breathing and slow down" (Janine Halloran, Coping Skills for Kids) [17]. A school OT practitioner says shape tracing "Provides a visual guide, which helps beginners practice deep breathing effectively" [21].
- *Common in apps.* "Most apps animate a breathing pattern of about 6 breaths per minute" (JMIR Serious Games, 2021) [7].

## Benefits and evidence

**A. Slow breathing in general (strongest evidence base, but not specific to visuals)**
- **Fincham et al. 2023, meta-analysis of RCTs (Sci Rep):** stress, k = 12, 785 adults, g = -0.35 (95% CI -0.55 to -0.14). Anxiety (k = 20) g = -0.32. Depressive symptoms (k = 18) g = -0.40. Most studies were at moderate risk of bias. The authors warn against "a miscalibration between hype and evidence" [2].
- **Laborde et al. 2022, systematic review and meta-analysis:** 223 studies. Voluntary slow breathing increased vagally mediated HRV during the session, right after one session, and after multi-session programmes. "Few adverse effects expected." [5]
- **Zaccaro et al. 2018, systematic review:** 15 studies of breathing under 10 breaths/min. Found increased HRV and RSA, and increased comfort, relaxation and alertness. Anxiety results were mixed. No RCTs; small samples; "a statistical pooling was infeasible" [4].
- **Resonance (~6/min):**
  - Maximum effects are usually seen near "0.1 Hz (six breaths per minute)". The authors refine this to about 5.5/min [3].
  - Individual resonance "ranges from 4.5 to 6.5 bpm for adults and 6.5 to 9.5 bpm for children". "Resonance frequency training does not always reward 6 bpm" [8].
- **HRV biofeedback (Goessl et al. 2017):** 24 studies, 484 participants. Large reduction in self-reported stress and anxiety (controlled g = 0.83). "More well-controlled studies are needed" [1].

**B. Studies that used a visual pacer specifically**
- **You et al. 2021:** 59 young adults followed a video of a ball moving up and down (4 s in / 6 s out, 6/min). RMSSD rose compared with a documentary control at 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes, with "no differences ... among the different session durations". Single sessions, healthy students [9].
- **Iwabe et al. 2025:** 17 students followed "visual cues" at 4 s in / 6 s out for 5 min. State anxiety was lower than with normal breathing (STAI-S 36.3 vs 40.9 post-task). RMSSD was lower immediately after, then no different. Small sample, not preregistered [30].
- **Lukic et al. 2021:** 16 adults. A standard circle animation brought breathing to 5.99/min (baseline 12.23). RMSSD rose from 45.97 to 81.86 [7].
- **Aritzeta et al. 2022 (children 7–11):** five 15-minute sessions with a moving ball pacer at 6 breaths/min. "High coherence" HRV rose from 23.04 to 79.52; controls did not change. Quasi-experimental, with internal inconsistencies in the paper [34].
- **Laborde et al. 2021/22 (n = 112):** slow breathing at 6/min without biofeedback had mostly similar effects to slow breathing with HRV biofeedback. Only emotional valence differed [6].

**C. Visual vs audio vs biofeedback (mixed, small studies)**
- Metronome vs visual waveform (n = 20): 86% found the audio harder. Physiology was broadly similar, and one HRV metric was higher with visual cues than with spontaneous breathing [10].
- Audio guidance "was more effective than visual at creating self-reported calm"; visual "led to more respiratory change but less subjective calm" (UIST 2012-era study; sample size not stated) [11].
- Chittaro & Sioni 2014 compared three app designs and reported that a visualisation improved on audio-only instructions [12]. Sample size and measures UNVERIFIED (full text not accessible).
- Adding sensor biofeedback to a visual guide (n = 40) improved breathing accuracy but lowered self-reported relaxation, "possibly due to higher cognitive workload" [35].

**D. Children, classroom and box breathing**
- Khng 2017: RCT with 122 Primary 5 students. Deep breathing before a maths test reduced self-reported anxiety and improved test performance [31a].
- Balban et al. 2023: 5 min/day for 28 days. Box breathing (n = 21) did **not** significantly beat mindfulness meditation on mood, anxiety, respiratory rate or HRV. Cyclic sighing did best on mood and respiratory rate [36a].
- Shape techniques themselves (star, lazy 8, triangle) have **no outcome studies found**. TLC for Kids notes research on figure 8 breathing "is limited" [14].

## Who it suits

Listed only where a source says so:
- **People of all ages, including children, parents and carers; patients before medical procedures** [15].
- **Beginners:** "helps beginners practice deep breathing effectively" [21].
- **People who find counting hard:** no counting or timing needed [14][15]. A Reddit user said counting and holding breaths makes their health anxiety worse (unverified mirror) [43].
- **Children and classrooms:**
  - Breathing ball for self-regulation in class [13].
  - Square videos "perfect to pop on just after lunch" [32].
  - Shape posters [17].
  - Pace slower and lower for age: 8–9 cycles/min for ages 6–11 [13]. Children's resonance range is 6.5–9.5 bpm [8].
- **Autistic students:** the Texas Education Agency autism toolkit says to "Provide students with a breathing guide before they need it" and post visuals in cool-down spaces. It also says "Some students may benefit from breathing apps, guided audio, or movement-based breathing" and "Adapt as needed." [22]
- **ADHD:**
  - An AuDHD psychologist writes that many breathing exercises "can be too long for our attention span" and suggests visual imagery [44].
  - No peer-reviewed study of visual pacers in ADHD was found (UNVERIFIED that it helps).
- **Breathlessness, anxiety, panic (adults):** rectangle breathing [23].
- **People with mild to moderate anxiety:** apps "can be helpful in moments of mild to moderate anxiety". People having panic attacks "might want to find a licensed professional" (therapist quoted in Mic) [45].

## How to use a breathing animation

- **Posture:**
  - "Standing up, sitting in a chair that supports your back, or lying on a bed or yoga mat"; feet flat, hip-width apart; loosen tight clothing [46].
  - Optional hand on chest and hand on belly; belly rises on the in-breath [9][29].
- **Nose vs mouth (sources differ):**
  - NHS: "Try breathing in through your nose and out through your mouth" [46].
  - Lab pacer studies: in through the nose, out through pursed lips [9].
  - Breathing ball: nose in and out [13].
  - Why the nose: it filters and humidifies air and releases nitric oxide. Mouth breathing is fine when the nose is blocked [47].
- **Speed:**
  - Adults often around 6/min (for example 4 s in / 6 s out) [9][3], but the individual best rate varies (4.5–6.5 adults) [8].
  - Shaffer & Meehan recommend a longer out-breath than in-breath [8].
  - Children faster (8–9/min ages 6–11) [13].
  - Start shorter if needed (2–3 s instead of 4 for box) [29].
  - "Stay at your comfort level ... not straining" [18].
- **Duration:**
  - NHS: "Repeat for at least 5 minutes"; daily practice gives most benefit [46].
  - Harvard: "at least 20 minutes a day, although even just a few minutes can help" (relaxation techniques generally) [25].
  - One lab study found 5 minutes raised RMSSD as much as 20 minutes [9].
  - Box breathing: 3–4 rounds, once or twice a day [18].
- **Breath size:** if you feel faint, "take shallower and smoother breaths" [8]. NHS: breathe in "as far as is comfortable, without forcing it" [46].
- **When to teach kids:** practise when calm, not mid-meltdown [19][22].

## Safety

- Slow breathing has "few adverse effects expected" [5].
- **Overbreathing:**
  - "Slower breathing may be difficult for some clients, they may overbreathe and expel excessive CO2"; watch for faintness [8].
  - Very slow rates (4.5 bpm) "may result in overbreathing" [8].
- **Medical conditions:**
  - Breath focus "may not be appropriate for those with health problems that make breathing difficult, such as respiratory ailments or heart failure" [25].
  - Resonance assessment is contraindicated with a pacemaker, and possibly with conditions involving blood acidosis such as kidney disease [8].
- **Relaxation can raise anxiety for some people.** In a sample of 300 students, "Approximately 15% (n=46) of individuals reported RIA". Asthma and generalised anxiety predicted it [28]. Trying to control breathing can make some people "more worried about their breath" [45].
- **Breath holds** (box, triangle) are optional in many guides. Pregnancy, heart and lung conditions: NOT RESEARCHED from an authoritative source (UNVERIFIED blog claims only).
- **Not a treatment for anxiety disorders.** Evidence is modest and risk of bias is moderate [2].
- **Children:** young children need guidance; consider "smaller lung capacity" [32].

## Myths vs facts

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| Everyone should breathe at exactly 6 breaths a minute. | Resonance varies: 4.5–6.5 bpm for adults, 6.5–9.5 bpm for children [8]. |
| Longer sessions are always better. | In one study, 5 minutes raised HRV as much as 20 minutes in a single session [9]. NHS suggests at least 5 minutes [46]. |
| Deep breathing means taking huge breaths. | Overbreathing can cause faintness; take shallower, smoother breaths [8]. Don't force it [46]. |
| Box breathing is proven better than other methods. | In a 28-day RCT, box breathing did not significantly beat mindfulness; cyclic sighing did best [36a]. |
| Visual guides are always better than audio. | Mixed. Visual cues were easier to follow for 86% in one study [10], but audio was more calming in another [11]. |
| Breathing exercises always relax you. | About 15% of a student sample reported relaxation-induced anxiety [28]. |
| Breathing apps can replace therapy for panic. | A therapist advises a licensed professional for panic attacks [45]. The meta-analysis effect is small-to-medium [2]. |
| Star breathing is about tracing a star. | Sometimes. Conscious Discipline's S.T.A.R. is an acronym [27], and Teach Starter uses "star" for hand tracing [32]. |
| Kids should breathe at adult speed. | Recommended rates are faster for ages 6–11 (8–9 cycles/min) [13]. |

**What they offer:** a single technique, usually box/square; a fixed-pace video or GIF; kid-focused printables; short "instant calm" clips.

**What they miss:**
- Most offer one shape only, with no choice of shapes in one place.
- Fixed pace with no way to slow down or speed up.
- Almost none explain *why* following a moving shape helps (no counting, attention anchor) or cite evidence.
- Little or no safety guidance (dizziness or overbreathing, relaxation-induced anxiety, breathing or heart conditions) [18][29][31][55].
- No age-specific pacing for kids. Children's Wisconsin is the exception [13].
- No note that ~6/min isn't right for everyone [8].
- Contradictory nose/mouth advice is not explained.
- Few separate the evidence for slow breathing from the evidence for visual pacing.
- Kids' content is mostly static printables, while adult content is mostly videos.
