Breathing Exercise for Panic Attack: A Step-by-Step Guide

Slow, gentle breathing may help ease a panic attack. Here is how to do it, why it works, and when to get help.

Quick answer

A widely recommended breathing exercise for a panic attack is slow, gentle belly breathing. Breathe in through your nose, out through your mouth, and count to five on each breath for at least 5 minutes. It may help ease symptoms such as a racing heart and dizziness by calming your body's fight-or-flight response.

It will not work for everyone every time, and it is not a substitute for medical care. Panic attacks are common: Cleveland Clinic reports that up to 11% of people in the United States have one each year.

Is it a panic attack or an emergency?

Panic symptoms can feel like a heart attack. The National Institute of Mental Health lists a pounding heart, chest pain, trouble breathing and dizziness among them. Do not wait to see if the symptoms go away if you are unsure.

  • Pain that spreads to the arm, jaw or neck, pressure or squeezing pain, symptoms that start after exertion, or pain that will not go away point toward a heart attack (Cleveland Clinic).
  • Chest pain, trouble breathing or loss of consciousness need emergency care.
  • If you are in doubt, call your local emergency number right away (Healthline).

How to do a breathing exercise during a panic attack

These steps combine advice from the NHS and Cleveland Clinic.

  1. Stay where you are. Sit or lie down if you can, and loosen any tight clothes.
  2. Place one hand on your belly and one on your chest. Your belly hand should move as you breathe, and your chest hand should stay mostly still.
  3. Breathe in gently through your nose. Let the breath flow down into your belly as far as is comfortable, without forcing it. If it helps, count slowly from 1 to 5.
  4. Breathe out gently through your mouth. Count from 1 to 5 again.
  5. Repeat for at least 5 minutes, or until you feel calmer. The NHS recommends at least 5 minutes.
  6. Remind yourself it will pass. The NHS advises telling yourself that the attack is not life threatening, and Rethink Mental Illness suggests that you try not to fight it.

Sources differ on how deep the breath should be. Slow, gentle and comfortable matters more than big. Researchers who treat panic warn that large, forced breaths can keep overbreathing going and make symptoms stronger (Meuret et al., 2010).

Why does breathing help with a panic attack?

During a panic attack your breathing speeds up, which is a sign that your body is in fight-or-flight mode. Psychologist Regina Josell tells Cleveland Clinic that slow breathing helps to turn that response off.

Breathing too fast also lowers the carbon dioxide level in your blood. Cleveland Clinic explains that this narrows blood vessels and can cause lightheadedness, tingling, chest pain and a pounding heart, which overlap with the symptoms of panic. MedlinePlus says the home-care goal is to raise the carbon dioxide level in your blood. In one trial, people with panic disorder who trained in slow, shallow breathing did raise their carbon dioxide levels (Meuret et al., 2010).

How long does a panic attack last?

The NHS says most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes, and some have been reported to last up to an hour. The HSE in Ireland gives a range of 5 to 30 minutes. Symptoms usually peak within 10 minutes, according to Cleveland Clinic.

What other breathing techniques can help?

If simple counted breathing does not suit you, these options are also described by reputable sources. Breath holds are not part of the NHS or HSE methods, so skip them if holding your breath feels uncomfortable.

  • Pursed-lip breathing. Pucker your lips as if you are blowing out a candle and breathe out slowly (Cleveland Clinic).
  • Box breathing. Breathe out fully, then in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4 and hold for 4. Do 3 to 4 rounds without straining (Cleveland Clinic). Read the full box breathing guide.
  • 4-7-8 breathing. Breathe in through your nose for 4, hold for 7 and breathe out through pursed lips for 8. You may feel lightheaded while you learn it (Cleveland Clinic). See the 4-7-8 breathing guide.
  • Cyclic sighing. Breathe in through your nose, take a second sip of air to fill your lungs, then breathe out very slowly through your mouth (Balban et al., 2023).
  • The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique. Name 5 things you see, 4 you touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell and 1 you taste. It is often paired with slow breathing (Cleveland Clinic).

Does a breathing exercise for a panic attack really work?

The honest answer is that it helps some people, and the evidence for panic specifically is weaker than the advice sounds.

  • Stress and anxiety in general: moderate evidence. A 2023 meta-analysis of 12 randomised trials with 785 adults found that breathwork lowered self-reported stress, with a similar small effect on anxiety. Most studies had a moderate risk of bias, and no clinical panic trials were included (Fincham et al.).
  • Panic disorder: weak or mixed evidence. A larger review found that breathing retraining added only small effects within cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) (Pompoli et al.).
  • Cyclic sighing: promising but limited. In a Stanford study, it improved mood more than mindfulness in healthy volunteers, but anxiety fell by a similar amount in every group. It was not tested in people with panic disorder or during an attack (Balban et al.).
  • The best-supported treatment is CBT. The National Institute of Mental Health calls it the gold standard for panic disorder.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Gulping big, fast breaths. This can keep overbreathing going. Keep each breath slow and gentle.
  • Forcing or straining. The NHS says to breathe without forcing it.
  • Using a paper bag. It is not recommended, especially with heart or lung problems.
  • Only trying it in the middle of an attack. The NHS says you get the most benefit from regular practice.
  • Assuming chest pain is just panic. If you are unsure, get it checked.

Panic attack breathing: myths and facts

  • Myth: breathing into a paper bag helps. Fact: there is a general lack of scientific evidence that it works (Mental Floss), and a case series reported deaths in patients whose low oxygen or heart problems were missed (Callaham, 1989).
  • Myth: breathlessness means you are short of oxygen. Fact: during panic, the symptoms are linked to low carbon dioxide from breathing too fast. Breathlessness can also have medical causes, so get checked if you are unsure (MedlinePlus).
  • Myth: you should always take a deep breath. Fact: sources disagree, and slow and gentle matters more than deep.
  • Myth: breathing exercises cure panic disorder. Fact: they add little within CBT, and CBT is the gold standard treatment.

How can you practise before the next panic attack?

Breathing is easier to use during an attack if you have practised when you are calm. A few minutes a day builds the habit, so the steps feel familiar when you need them. A guided pace gives you something to follow instead of counting.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a panic attack last?

The NHS says most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes, and some have been reported to last up to an hour. The HSE in Ireland gives a range of 5 to 30 minutes. Symptoms usually peak within 10 minutes, according to Cleveland Clinic.

Can a breathing exercise stop a panic attack?

Slow, gentle breathing is widely recommended to help ease the symptoms, but it is not guaranteed to stop an attack. Research on breathing exercises for panic disorder specifically is weak or mixed, so think of it as one coping tool rather than a cure.

Can you die from a panic attack?

The NHS says panic attacks are frightening but not dangerous. However, panic symptoms can feel like a heart attack. If you are unsure, or this is your first time, get emergency care.

How can I tell a panic attack from a heart attack?

They can feel alike. Pain that spreads to the arm, jaw or neck, pressure or squeezing pain, symptoms that start after exertion, and pain that will not go away point toward a heart attack. When in doubt, call emergency services.

Does breathing into a paper bag help?

It is not recommended. There is a general lack of scientific evidence that it works, and a case series reported deaths in patients whose low oxygen or heart problems were missed. Avoid it, especially if you have heart or lung problems.

Can deep breathing make a panic attack worse?

It can for some people. Researchers warn that big, forced breaths can keep overbreathing going and intensify symptoms. Slow, gentle breaths matter more than deep ones.

When should I see a doctor about panic attacks?

See a GP or doctor if attacks happen regularly. Treatments include cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), which the National Institute of Mental Health calls the gold standard, and medication. If you have thoughts of suicide in the US, call or text 988.
