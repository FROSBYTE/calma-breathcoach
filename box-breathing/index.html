Box Breathing: How to Do It, With a Guided Animation

Four equal steps: in, hold, out, hold. Follow along, then see what the research says.

Quick answer

Box breathing is a slow breathing pattern with four equal steps: breathe in, hold, breathe out, hold, each for a count of four. Picture tracing the four sides of a box as you go. One round takes about 16 seconds, it needs no equipment, and you can do it almost anywhere.[1]

What is box breathing?

Box breathing is also called square breathing, four-square breathing, 4x4 or 4-4-4-4 breathing. Cleveland Clinic describes it as a yogic deep-breathing technique, known in yoga as sama vritti pranayama, in which each part of the breath is the same length.[2]

The name “box breathing” was popularised by former Navy SEAL Mark Divine, who told TIME he developed it “to continue my breath training after the SEALs”.[3] Similar four-count “combat” or “tactical” breathing has been taught in police and military training.[4]

Many pages say Navy SEALs use it, but we could not confirm that from an official Navy source, so we do not claim it. Note too that “tactical breathing” is not always box breathing: the Canadian Armed Forces version is in for 4, out for 4, with no holds.[5]

How do you do box breathing?

Sit upright with your back supported and your feet flat on the floor. You can also stand or lie on your back.[6, 7] If it helps, rest one hand on your chest and one on your stomach to check that your belly moves as you breathe.[7]

  1. Breathe out slowly and fully to empty your lungs.[2, 6]
  2. Breathe in through your nose for a count of 4.[2]
  3. Hold for a count of 4. Keep it gentle and do not clamp your throat or nose shut.[8, 3]
  4. Breathe out slowly for a count of 4.[2]
  5. Hold with your lungs empty for a count of 4, then begin the next round.[2]

Repeat for three or four rounds.[2] Sources differ on whether to breathe out through the nose or the mouth, so use whichever feels natural.[6, 3]

What if the count feels too long?

The holds are the hardest part for many people. You can adjust the count without losing the pattern:

  • Shorten each step to 2 or 3 seconds while you are learning.[7, 1] A 3-3-3-3 count suits some people better.[4]
  • Shorten or drop the holds and focus on slow, steady breathing if they feel uncomfortable.[9, 10]

Is box breathing safe? When to get help

This page is general information, not medical advice. Box breathing is not a treatment for anxiety disorders or any medical condition.

  • Healthline calls box breathing “generally safe”. Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition, a severe health condition or you are pregnant.[6] Medical News Today also lists high blood pressure.[8]
  • Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy or short of breath, and get medical help if symptoms do not go away.[6]
  • If the holds make you feel more anxious or short of breath, shorten them or skip them and just breathe slowly. If you are prone to panic, our panic relief breathing guide may suit you better.
  • If anxiety or panic is affecting your daily life, talk to a doctor or other health professional.

How long and how often should you practise?

Advice varies, so start small:

  • Cleveland Clinic suggests 3 to 4 rounds, once or twice a day.[2]
  • Pulmonologist Dr. Ni-Cheng Liang advises no more than three to five breath cycles at a time, because beginners may get dizzy.[11]
  • In the 2023 Stanford trial, people practised for 5 minutes a day for 28 days.[12]

Start with three or four rounds, add time slowly, and practise when you are calm, not only in a stressful moment.[2, 13]

How does box breathing calm you down?

  • Slow breathing. Cleveland Clinic says box breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system, the body’s “rest and digest” side.[2] A review of 15 studies linked slow breathing (under 10 breaths a minute) with higher heart rate variability and with more comfort and relaxation and less anxiety, depression and anger. The reviewers say the mechanisms are still under debate.[14]
  • Counting gives your mind a focus. Cleveland Clinic compares silently counting to mantra meditation. This is an expert view, not something tested in trials.[2]

Does box breathing work? What the research says

Short answer: small studies suggest it can ease stress and anxiety in the short term for healthy people. The evidence is limited, and most studies were small and ran for a short time.

  • Stanford, 2023. In a randomised trial of 108 people split across four breathing and meditation groups (21 of them assigned to box breathing), five minutes a day for 28 days improved mood and anxiety in the box breathing group. But only cyclic sighing did significantly better than mindfulness meditation.[12]
  • Public speaking test, 2026. In a randomised trial of 66 students, box breathing reduced the rise in heart rate, state anxiety and a saliva stress marker during a virtual public-speaking test. Heart rate variability and cortisol did not differ between groups.[15, 16]
  • One minute in real life, 2026. In a pilot study of 47 students, one minute of box breathing during real stressful moments reduced state anxiety more than the control condition, though reaction times were slower afterwards.[17]

What is not shown:

  • Box breathing has not been shown to raise heart rate variability, and the claims that it lowers cortisol or blood pressure usually lean on research into other kinds of breathing.[12, 15]
  • We found no trial of box breathing for sleep.
  • After hard exercise, it was not better than slow breathing at six breaths a minute. In a study of 40 sports-science students, box breathing left heart rate higher and felt harder.[18]

Breathwork in general shows a similar picture. A 2023 meta-analysis found lower stress, anxiety and depression on average, but the effect on stress was not significant in people with mental or physical health conditions.[19]

When should you use box breathing?

  • Before a test, speech or presentation. Dr. Liang suggests it for staying focused and alert but calm,[11] and it blunted the stress response before a public-speaking task in one trial.[15]
  • In a stressful moment, at work, at home or in public.[7] One minute reduced state anxiety in the student pilot above.[17]
  • Not straight after hard exercise. Slower breathing worked better in the study above.[18]
  • For sleep, there is no box breathing trial. For winding down, experts more often suggest 4-7-8 breathing[11, 20]; see also our breathing for sleep guide.

Common mistakes and tips

  • Clamping or straining. Hold gently without clamping the mouth or nose, and do not force the breath.[8, 3, 13]
  • Doing too many rounds at first. Stick to three to five at a time to avoid dizziness.[11]
  • Shallow chest breathing. Rest a hand on your stomach and check that it moves.[7]
  • Pushing through uncomfortable holds. Shorten or skip them.[9, 10]

Box breathing myths vs facts

MythFact
Navy SEALs invented box breathing.The equal four-part breath comes from yoga’s sama vritti. Mark Divine popularised the name and told TIME he developed it after the SEALs.[2, 3]
The Stanford study proved box breathing is the best technique.Cyclic sighing did best. Box breathing improved mood and anxiety within its group but did not significantly beat mindfulness meditation.[12]
It boosts heart rate variability or lowers cortisol.Not shown for box breathing in the main trials.[12, 15]
It treats anxiety disorders.Studies were mostly in healthy people and students, and the Stanford trial excluded moderate to severe psychiatric conditions.[12, 19]

No trial has compared the two head to head, so the better choice depends on what you need. Read our full guide to 4-7-8 breathing.

Two other options: cyclic sighing (a double inhale and a long exhale), which did best for mood in the Stanford trial,[12] and the NHS stress exercise, which has no holds.[13]

Box breathing FAQ

What is box breathing?

Box breathing is a slow breathing pattern with four equal steps: breathe in, hold, breathe out, hold, usually for a count of four each. It is also called square breathing or 4-4-4-4 breathing, and it is rooted in the yoga practice of sama vritti.

How long should I do box breathing?

Start with three or four rounds, once or twice a day. Each round takes about 16 seconds at a count of four. Beginners can get dizzy, so add time slowly.

Is box breathing good for anxiety?

Small studies in healthy people and students found it can reduce anxiety in the short term. It is not a treatment for anxiety disorders, so talk to a health professional if anxiety affects your daily life.

Is box breathing safe? Who should avoid it?

Medical sites call it generally safe. Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, a lung condition or you are pregnant, and stop if you feel dizzy. If the holds feel uncomfortable, shorten or skip them.

Is box breathing the same as Navy SEAL tactical breathing?

Not always. Former Navy SEAL Mark Divine popularised the name box breathing, but the equal four-part breath comes from yoga, and the Canadian Armed Forces version of tactical breathing has no holds.

Can box breathing help me sleep?

We found no trial testing box breathing for sleep, and a 2023 Stanford trial found no changes in sleep. For winding down, experts more often suggest 4-7-8 breathing, which has a longer exhale.
