Breathing Exercises for Sleep: A Calm, Practical Guide

Simple steps for falling asleep, calming a busy mind and getting back to sleep.

Quick answer

Breathing exercises for sleep work by slowing your breath so your body and mind can settle before bed. The simplest version is to breathe in gently, breathe out a little more slowly, and keep going for at least five minutes without forcing anything [1]. The research is promising but still small, so think of breathing as one helpful part of a good bedtime routine, not a cure for insomnia [2].

How do you do breathing exercises for sleep?

Two gentle methods are enough for most people. Try one for a week before you switch.

Slow, steady breathing

  1. Get comfortable. Lying in bed or sitting both work [1].
  2. Breathe in slowly and count from 1 to 5.
  3. Breathe out slowly and count from 1 to 5. Do not force it.
  4. Keep going for at least 5 minutes [1].

Sleep studies of slow breathing aim for about 6 breaths a minute. One protocol was to breathe in for 4.5 seconds and out through pursed lips for 5.5 seconds, for 15 minutes each evening over 30 days [3].

Belly (diaphragmatic) breathing

  1. Put one hand on your chest and one on your belly [4].
  2. Breathe in through your nose so your belly rises and your chest stays still [4].
  3. Breathe out and let your belly fall.
  4. Repeat for 20 breaths [4], or for a few minutes when you get into bed [5]. Cleveland Clinic suggests learning it lying down first [6].

Why do breathing exercises help you fall asleep?

Trouble falling asleep is often about being too switched on. Researchers call this presleep arousal: the mental and physiological activation people experience when they try to fall asleep [7]. One explanation of insomnia is “hyperarousal”, a “tired but wired” state. In that view, slow breathing may raise vagal (parasympathetic) tone, but this is a hypothesis, not a proven finding [8].

A review of 15 studies linked slow breathing (fewer than 10 breaths per minute) with higher heart rate variability (HRV), more relaxation and less anxiety. The authors call the evidence “interesting albeit limited” [9].

Do breathing exercises really work for sleep?

They may help, and the evidence is still early. Here is what the studies found:

  • A 2026 review of 9 studies (457 participants) found people reported better sleep after slow breathing before bed, but the wrist-sensor and sleep-lab measurements were inconclusive [2].
  • In a small study of 14 people with self-reported insomnia, paced breathing was linked to falling asleep faster, fewer awakenings and better sleep efficiency [10].
  • In a 30-day study of 64 people, sleep-quality scores (PSQI, where lower is better) went from 3.31 to 2.91 in the breathing group and from 3.44 to 3.75 in the control group [3].
  • One night of slow breathing in 20 people did not give a clear effect: 12.56 minutes to fall asleep versus 16.52, which was not statistically significant [7].
  • The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends relaxation therapy, which includes breathing, only conditionally, based on “a small body of low quality evidence”. CBT-I is its strong, first-line recommendation for chronic insomnia [11].

Breathing is low-risk and worth trying. Give it a few weeks: regular use is usually more effective than one-off use [5].

Which breathing exercise is best to fall asleep fast?

No technique has been proven best. These are the common options:

  • Slow or belly breathing. The simplest. Start here.
  • A longer breath out. Common advice, but the evidence is mixed. At 6 breaths per minute, one study found no HRV difference between a 1:2 and a 1:1 breathe-in to breathe-out ratio [12]. Cyclic sighing (two breaths in through the nose, then a long breath out) improved mood in another study, but its sleep measures did not change [13].
  • 4-7-8 breathing. Breathe in for 4, hold for 7, breathe out for 8. Dr Andrew Weil's guidance is “do not do more than four breaths at one time for the first month”, and some people feel lightheaded [14]. We found no published trial results on 4-7-8 and sleep [15]. Full steps are in our 4-7-8 breathing guide.
  • Box breathing. Breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4 [4]. Skip long holds if they feel uncomfortable [5]. See our box breathing guide.

How do I calm racing thoughts at night?

The AASM describes relaxation therapy as targeting both body tension and “cognitive arousal” [11]. Harvard Health says breath focus means gently shifting your attention away from distracting thoughts [16]. Three things to try:

  • Body scan. After a few deep breaths, move your attention from your feet up to your forehead and release tension in each area [5] [16].
  • Progressive muscle relaxation. Tense each muscle group for 5 to 10 seconds, then pause for 10 to 20 seconds before the next group [5]. Harvard suggests lying on your back, breathing slowly and working upward from the feet [17].
  • Stop trying so hard. “The harder you try to sleep, the more difficult it is to fall asleep” [18]. Use breathing to rest, not to make yourself sleep.

How do I build a bedtime breathing routine?

  • Start winding down “anywhere between 30 minutes to 2 hours before bed” and put electronics away at the start [19]. The NHS suggests avoiding phones, tablets and computers for an hour before bed [20].
  • Go to bed and get up at similar times every day [20] [21].
  • Practise most nights. Studies used 15 to 30 minutes a night for about 4 weeks [2] [3]. A few minutes in bed is a fine start [5].
  • Lying down or sitting both work [1].

What should I do if I wake up at 3am?

“Waking during the night is actually a normal part of sleep.” Sleep cycles last 90 to 110 minutes [22].

  • Use slow, deep breathing. Avoid clock-watching and screens [23].
  • If you are still awake after about 15 to 20 minutes, get up, do something relaxing elsewhere and return to bed when you feel sleepy [23] [5].
  • Frequent waking can be a sign of insomnia or sleep apnoea [24].

What are the common mistakes?

  • Expecting instant results [5].
  • Forcing the breath [1].
  • Doing long breath holds when they feel uncomfortable [5].
  • Turning the exercise into an effort to make yourself sleep [18].
  • Checking the clock [23].
  • Ignoring insomnia that lasts for months, or signs of sleep apnoea [21] [25].

Myths vs facts about breathing and sleep

  • Myth: the military method sends you to sleep in two minutes. Fact: the “96 percent… two minutes” claim comes from Bud Winter's book, after six weeks of practice. We found no peer-reviewed test of it [26].
  • Myth: you should fall asleep instantly. Fact: 10 to 20 minutes is normal, and falling asleep within a minute may suggest sleep deprivation [27] [28].
  • Myth: breathing exercises cure insomnia. Fact: they are an adjunct [29]. CBT-I is the first-line treatment [11].
  • Myth: studies have proven that breathing improves sleep. Fact: the objective evidence is inconclusive [2].

When should you see a doctor about sleep?

This page is general information, not medical advice.

  • See a GP if changing your habits has not helped, you have had trouble sleeping for months, or it affects your daily life. Long-term insomnia means 3 months or longer [21].
  • CBT-I is the first-line treatment, usually 4 to 8 sessions [30]. The NHS says a GP may offer CBT face to face or as an online self-help programme [21].
  • Sleep apnoea warning signs: breathing that stops and starts, gasping or choking, loud snoring, daytime tiredness and morning headaches [25].
  • Check with a doctor before relaxation exercises if you have epilepsy, a psychiatric condition or a history of trauma [5], respiratory problems or heart failure [16], or COPD, asthma or anxiety [6].
  • Stop if you feel lightheaded. Focusing on the breath can raise anxiety in some people [31].

Frequently asked questions

What are the best breathing exercises for sleep?

Slow breathing and belly (diaphragmatic) breathing are the simplest to start with. Other options include 4-7-8, box breathing and a longer breath out. No single technique has been proven best, so choose the one that feels easiest to do without effort.

How long should you do breathing exercises before bed?

The NHS suggests at least 5 minutes of slow breathing. Studies used 15 to 30 minutes a night for about 4 weeks. A few minutes in bed is a fine place to start, and doing it regularly matters more than doing it for a long time.

Can breathing exercises cure insomnia?

No. Breathing exercises are described as an adjunct, which means an add-on to other treatment. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia.

Why does focusing on my breathing make me more anxious?

For some people, paying close attention to the breath can raise anxiety. Try breathing gently without forcing it, follow a visual guide so you do not have to count, and speak to a doctor if it keeps happening.

How long should it take to fall asleep?

Around 10 to 20 minutes is normal. Falling asleep within a minute can suggest sleep deprivation.

Should I get out of bed if I cannot sleep?

If you are still awake after about 15 to 20 minutes, get up, do something relaxing somewhere else and go back to bed when you feel sleepy. Avoid checking the clock and avoid screens.
