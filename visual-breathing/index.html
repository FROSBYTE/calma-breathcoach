Breathing Animation: A Visual Breathing Exercise You Can Follow

A breathing animation is a moving shape that sets your pace. You breathe in as it grows or rises and out as it shrinks or falls, so your eyes handle the timing and you don't need to count.

Quick answer

Many people find a visual breathing exercise easier than counting seconds in their head. The shape does the timekeeping, and you simply follow it. This page explains how to use a breathing animation, shows what breathing shapes look like, and sets out what the research does and doesn't say. It is general information, not medical advice.

How do you do a visual breathing exercise?

  1. Get comfortable. The NHS suggests standing, sitting in a chair that supports your back, or lying on a bed or yoga mat, with your feet flat and loose clothing around your waist [10].
  2. Watch the shape. Breathe in as the animation grows or rises. Breathe out as it shrinks or falls [8].
  3. Choose how you breathe. The NHS suggests breathing in through your nose and out through your mouth [10]. Lab studies of paced breathing often use the nose in and pursed lips out [2]. Sources differ, so pick what feels comfortable.
  4. Keep each breath smooth, not huge. The NHS advises breathing in as far as is comfortable, without forcing it [10]. If you feel faint, take shallower and smoother breaths [6].
  5. Carry on for a few minutes. The NHS says to repeat for at least 5 minutes [10]. Harvard Health says that for relaxation techniques in general, even a few minutes can help [21].

How fast should the breathing shape move?

Adults often breathe at around six breaths a minute with a pacer, and research on breathing and heart rate variability usually finds the biggest effects near 0.1 Hz, which is six breaths per minute [7]. In one lab study, people followed a ball that moved for 4 seconds in and 6 seconds out [2]. But the best rate varies from person to person. One review gives a range of 4.5 to 6.5 breaths per minute for adults and 6.5 to 9.5 for children [6]. If a pace feels rushed or strained, slow down or choose a gentler one. Comfort matters more than hitting an exact number.

Why does following a moving shape help?

  • You don't have to count. A children's charity describes its guided breathing as having "no need to count or worry about timing" [8].
  • It can be easier to follow than sound. In one study of 20 people, 86% said breathing to a metronome was harder than following visual cues [4].
  • It gives your attention somewhere to rest. Children's Wisconsin says a breathing ball "serves as a focus, or an anchor, for mindful breathing" [11]. Following the shape with your eyes gives your mind "something gentle to focus on" [9].

Does a visual breathing guide really work?

There are two separate questions here: does slow breathing help, and does a visual guide add anything. The evidence for the first is better than for the second.

Slow breathing in general

A 2023 meta-analysis of 12 randomised trials with 785 adults found a small-to-medium effect on stress (g = -0.35). Most of the studies had a moderate risk of bias, and the authors warn against "a miscalibration between hype and evidence" [1].

Visual pacers specifically

  • In a 2021 study, 59 young adults followed a video of a ball moving up and down at 4 seconds in and 6 seconds out. A heart rate variability measure (RMSSD) rose compared with a documentary control. These were single sessions in healthy students [2].
  • In a 2025 study, 17 students followed visual cues for 5 minutes. Their state anxiety scores were lower than with normal breathing (36.3 versus 40.9). The sample was small [3].
  • Visual versus audio guidance is mixed. Another small study found audio guidance produced more self-reported calm than visual guidance [5].
  • Shape techniques such as star, lazy 8 and triangle breathing have no outcome studies that we could find. One charity notes that research on figure 8 breathing "is limited" [9].

The fair summary: following a paced visual guide is promising and low-risk for most people, but the research on shapes themselves is thin.

Who is a visual breathing exercise for?

  • Beginners. A school occupational therapy practitioner says shape tracing "provides a visual guide, which helps beginners practice deep breathing effectively" [15].
  • People who find counting hard. Guides that need no counting remove that step [8].
  • Children and classrooms. Shapes are taught as a playful way to keep track of breathing [14]. Children's Wisconsin suggests 8 to 9 cycles a minute for ages 6 to 11 and 6 to 7 for ages 12 and older [11].
  • Autistic students. The Texas Education Agency's autism toolkit suggests giving students a breathing guide before they need it, and adapting it as needed [18].
  • Feeling breathless or anxious. NHS hospital guidance on rectangle breathing says it "may help if you are feeling breathless, anxious or panicky" [16]. For panic attacks, a therapist quoted by Mic advises finding a licensed professional [19].

Not medical advice: when to be careful

  • This page is general information. It does not diagnose or treat any condition, and breathing exercises do not replace care from a doctor or therapist.
  • Stop if you feel faint or dizzy, and return to normal breathing. Slower breathing can lead some people to overbreathe [6].
  • Relaxation can raise anxiety for some people. In a sample of 300 students, about 15% (46 people) reported relaxation-induced anxiety [20]. If focusing on your breath makes you more worried, stop and try something else.
  • Harvard Health notes that breath focus may not be appropriate for people with health problems that make breathing difficult, such as respiratory ailments or heart failure [21]. Check with your doctor if that applies to you.
  • If you have panic attacks, ongoing anxiety or any symptom that worries you, speak to a doctor or a licensed professional.

Which breathing shapes do people use, and what are the patterns?

Sources describe these patterns differently, so treat them as common versions rather than one correct method.

Expanding circle or ball

Breathe in as it expands and out as it contracts. Children's Wisconsin describes a steady four-count inhale, a slight pause and a four-count exhale [11].

Square (box breathing)

Cleveland Clinic: exhale fully first, then breathe in through your nose for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4 and hold for 4. Repeat for three to four rounds [12]. WebMD says if 4 seconds is too much, you can start with 2 or 3 [13]. See our guide to box breathing.

Triangle

A common children's version is 3-3-3: in for 3, hold for 3, out for 3 [14]. A hospital patient guide gives the same pattern [17].

Rectangle

Follow the sides with your eyes as you breathe, gradually slowing your eye movement to slow your breathing. Optional counts are in for 2 and out for 3, or in for 4 and out for 6 [16].

Lazy 8 (figure 8)

Start in the middle, trace the left loop as you breathe in and the right loop as you breathe out, with no counts [14].

Common myths about breathing guides

Myth: everyone should breathe at exactly six breaths a minute. Fact: the best rate varies, from 4.5 to 6.5 breaths per minute for adults in one review [6].

Myth: longer sessions are always better. Fact: in one study, 5 minutes raised RMSSD as much as 20 minutes in a single session [2].

Myth: box breathing is proven better than other methods. Fact: in a 28-day trial, box breathing did not significantly beat mindfulness meditation on mood, anxiety, respiratory rate or heart rate variability [22].

Questions people ask about visual breathing

Do I need to count when I follow a breathing animation?

Not usually. The shape sets the pace, so you breathe in as it grows or rises and out as it shrinks or falls. One children's charity describes this kind of guided breathing as having no need to count or worry about timing.

How long should I follow a breathing animation?

The NHS suggests repeating a breathing exercise for at least 5 minutes. Harvard Health says that for relaxation techniques in general, even a few minutes can help. In one lab study of young adults, 5 minutes raised a heart rate variability measure as much as 20 minutes did in a single session.

Is a visual breathing guide better than an audio guide?

The evidence is mixed and the studies are small. In one study of 20 people, 86% found breathing to a metronome harder than following visual cues. In another, audio guidance produced more self-reported calm than visual guidance. Choose whichever feels easier for you.

Can deep breathing make anxiety worse?

For some people, yes. In a sample of 300 students, about 15% reported relaxation-induced anxiety. If trying to control your breathing makes you more worried, stop and try something else, and consider speaking to a licensed professional.

Why do deep breaths make me dizzy?

Slower breathing can lead some people to overbreathe and breathe out too much carbon dioxide, which can cause faintness. If you feel faint, take shallower and smoother breaths. The NHS advises breathing in as far as is comfortable, without forcing it.

Is a breathing animation suitable for children?

Shape-based breathing is mostly taught to children by teachers, school counsellors and occupational therapy practitioners. Children's Wisconsin suggests 8 to 9 cycles a minute for ages 6 to 11, which is faster than the usual adult pace.
